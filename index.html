Story 1 of 4

Meet your endocrine system

Scroll slowly. Each gland lights up and sends its hormones off through the bloodstream.

01 · The big idea

Your body's wireless network

Endocrine glands are spread all over your body. Instead of wires, they send chemical messages called hormones through your bloodstream.

Hormones reach almost every organ, but only cells with the matching receptor respond.

02 · Command centre

Hypothalamus + pituitary

The hypothalamus links your brain to your hormones. It tells the pea-sized pituitary what to release.

Pituitary hormones then switch other glands on, like the thyroid, adrenals and gonads. That's why it's nicknamed the "master gland."

03 · The night shift

Pineal gland

A tiny gland deep in the brain that releases melatonin when it gets dark, helping set your sleep–wake rhythm.

04 · Metabolism dial

Thyroid

This butterfly-shaped gland in your neck makes T3 and T4, which help set how fast your cells use energy.

Fun fact: our logo turns the thyroid into a heart. 💚

05 · Stress responders

Adrenal glands

They sit on top of your kidneys. Adrenaline gives the quick "fight-or-flight" boost (racing heart, alert muscles). Cortisol handles the longer stress response.

06 · Sugar manager

Pancreas

Clusters of cells called islets release insulin, which lowers blood sugar, and glucagon, which raises it. Together they keep your fuel supply steady.

07 · Growing up

Ovaries & testes

The gonads make sex hormones such as estrogen, progesterone and testosterone, which guide puberty, reproduction, bone health and more.

08 · All together

Many glands, one system

Every gland is talking at once, all day, every day. That's endocrinology, and it's what EUSA is all about.

The kidneys, heart, stomach and even fat tissue make hormones too!

Insulin: the key to the cell

What happens to your blood sugar after a snack?

Blood glucose5.0mmol/L (illustrative)
glucose insulin
01 · Snack time

Sugar hits your blood

You eat a granola bar between lectures. As it's digested, glucose moves into your bloodstream and your blood sugar rises.

02 · The pancreas notices

Insulin is released

Beta cells in the pancreas sense the rise and release insulin into the blood.

03 · Lock and key

Insulin binds its receptor

Insulin fits into insulin receptors on muscle and fat cells. That signal sends glucose channels (called GLUT4) up to the cell surface.

04 · Back to normal

Glucose moves in

Glucose flows into the cells to be used or stored, and blood sugar drops back into range. This is negative feedback in action.

In type 1 diabetes the body makes little or no insulin. In type 2, cells respond less well to it.

Story 3 of 4

Stress mode: the HPA axis

Ever felt wired the night before a midterm? Here's the hormone chain behind it.

01 · Stressor

"Midterm tomorrow"

Your brain registers stress, and the hypothalamus kicks things off.

02 · H → P

CRH

The hypothalamus releases CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone), which signals the pituitary.

03 · P → A

ACTH

The pituitary releases ACTH into the blood. It travels down to the adrenal cortex.

04 · A → body

Cortisol

The adrenals release cortisol. It raises blood sugar for quick energy and helps your body handle the challenge.

05 · The off switch

Negative feedback

Cortisol tells the hypothalamus and pituitary to ease off, so the loop switches itself down.

Long-term stress can keep this loop running, which is why sleep and breaks matter during exams.

Story 4 of 4

Melatonin: the night shift

Scroll from afternoon to morning and watch your pineal gland clock in.

01 · 3 PM

Daylight = low melatonin

Light enters your eyes and signals your brain's master clock (the SCN) that it's daytime, so melatonin stays low.

02 · 9 PM

Darkness flips the switch

As it gets dark, the pineal gland starts releasing melatonin.

Bright screens late at night can hold this back and delay feeling sleepy.

03 · 3 AM

Peak night

Melatonin peaks in the middle of the night. It doesn't knock you out. It's a timing signal that tells your body "it's night."

04 · 7 AM

Morning reset

Morning light brings melatonin back down and keeps your circadian rhythm in sync. Read the full explainer →
